Day 1 Recap. The Investor Lens.

An investor-lens working tool, not a transcript. Read it with your own funnel in mind.

Hosted by Doug Novack. Space provided by William Blair. Cubs game sponsored by ServiceTitan.
Discussions facilitated by Mike Sabo and Jesse Zahoran.
Chatham House Rules: no names, no attribution, patterns only
DAY 1 RECAP

Capital Is Rotating Into Physical Services

  • Day 1 was hosted at William Blair, whose investment banking team opened the day with the PE-investor view of the home and commercial services market.
  • A recurring theme was that investors are reallocating out of pure software and tech into durable, field-based, unit-economic businesses.
  • The framing in the room was that home services are increasingly viewed as AI-enhanced rather than AI-disrupted, and that thesis is pulling fresh capital in.
  • Investors who built their playbooks in consumer products and food and beverage are bringing that sophistication to a sector they consider relatively untapped.
  • Net effect for operators: demand for high-quality home services platforms is rising, and the bar for what counts as a premium asset is rising with it.
  • The window for telling a clean, growth-led story is open right now.
DAY 1 RECAP

Organic Growth Is The Story; M&A Is Table Stakes

Investors no longer reward deal velocity. They reward an organic engine that outperforms the underlying market.

  • Several speakers emphasized that organic growth now accounts for the majority of buy-side due diligence on premium home services assets.
  • M&A is no longer the differentiator it was three to five years ago. It is expected, but it is treated more like a commodity capability than a value driver.
  • The strongest narrative in the market is a business outperforming its underlying market by two to three times, with sales and marketing as the engine.
  • A working target surfaced in the room: roughly 10 percent or better organic growth, which would be a 2-3x outperformance vs. the 3-5 percent annual growth typical of various home services sub-segments.
  • For attendees: every tuck-in becomes organic growth on day one of ownership. Do not let brokers or board decks understate the organic effort you are already running.
  • The takeaway is to lead with the durability of your organic engine, not the velocity of your deal pipeline.
DAY 1 RECAP

From Percent Of Revenue To Dollar In, Dollar Out

  • The clearest mental shift offered in the room was to stop benchmarking marketing as a percent of revenue and start treating it as a capital investment with a measurable return.
  • A useful frame that emerged: every marketing dollar should be evaluated like any other unit of investment in the business, with a clear answer to "what did this dollar bring back."
  • Investor diligence is moving in this direction. They want to understand the efficiency of each layer of the funnel before they care about the headline spend ratio.
  • The practical implication is that marketing leaders should reframe their internal narrative from cost center to steward of growth capital.
  • Doing this changes the conversation with the CFO, the board, and any future buyer.
DAY 1 RECAP

Channel Diversification Is A Resilience Strategy

Single-channel demand engines are getting riskier. Investors increasingly read mix as a sign of operating maturity.

  • Single-channel demand engines are getting riskier as paid digital becomes more expensive and less efficient.
  • The pattern that earned the most credibility: a mix of digital, organic, brand, door-to-door, and tech-generated leads, with the mix shifting as a market matures.
  • New markets often need higher-cost acquisition motions to build density, then shift toward organic and brand-driven leads over time.
  • Diversification is not just a hedge. It is what gives marketing the levers to respond to demand shocks, capacity shifts, and competitive pressure.
  • Investors increasingly read channel diversification as a sign of operating maturity.
DAY 1 RECAP

Attribution Earns The Right To Invest More

  • Investors want to see a credible attribution model before they will fund step-change marketing investment.
  • The bar is not perfection. The working threshold described in the room: 75 to 80 percent confidence on channel attribution is enough to act on.
  • The benefit of getting closer to that confidence is permission, internally and externally, to push more dollars through a funnel that is demonstrably working.
  • Where attribution typically breaks is in the middle of the funnel, between top-of-funnel source and booked job. That is where attendees flagged the most opportunity.
  • The work to close the attribution gap pays off twice: once in better internal decisions, once in higher-quality conversations with investors.
DAY 1 RECAP

Stitch The Funnel To The Service Board

Flex the accordion between marketing demand and operations supply on a rolling three-to-five-day window.

  • One useful frame that emerged was to manage demand generation in near-real-time against operational capacity, on a rolling three-to-five-day window.
  • Several operators described dialing spend up when the service board has slack and pulling back when technicians are fully booked, rather than running a static monthly budget.
  • The phrase that landed in the room was "flex the accordion" between marketing demand and operations supply.
  • Done well, this protects close rates, response times, and customer experience, all of which show up in investor diligence as quality of growth.
  • This is one of the highest-leverage operating habits a marketing leader can build with their ops counterpart.
DAY 1 RECAP

Sales Apparatus Is Part Of The Marketing Story

  • Top performers institutionalize a standardized in-home or in-call selling playbook, not just a lead generation engine.
  • Top-performing close rates discussed in the room cluster around 40 percent and above, driven by repeatable training, not by individual hero sellers.
  • The bridge between a service technician and a comfort advisor or sales specialist was flagged as a major lever in higher-ticket categories.
  • For marketing leaders, this means the funnel does not end at lead handoff. The conversion engine sits inside the marketing story buyers will scrutinize.
  • A useful internal test: can you describe your sales playbook with the same rigor as your channel mix.
DAY 1 RECAP

LTV and Cross-Sell Are The Next Frontier

  • The conversation on lifetime value sharpened noticeably, with route-based and recurring-service models leading.
  • Top-performing LTV to CAC ratios cited in the room tend to land around five times, with category-level variance.
  • Cross-sell into the existing customer base was repeatedly framed as a high-return path. Pest control adding lawn care, exterior services bundling together, and HVAC platforms extending into adjacent home services were the patterns cited.
  • The strategic question that emerged was less "should we cross-sell" and more "which adjacent services share enough of our operating model to add cleanly."
  • Membership models came up as a way to operationalize cross-sell and to lift LTV at the same time.
DAY 1 RECAP

Mono-Brand Mandate Is Dead; Equity Wins

Local brand equity is an asset to protect, not a constraint to remove. Operating consistency matters more than the logo on the truck.

  • A notable shift from prior years: investors are no longer pushing operators to consolidate everything under a single national brand.
  • The frame that landed was that local brand equity, where it exists, is an asset to protect, not a constraint to remove.
  • The harder question is operating consistency across a family of brands. That is what investors care about, more than the logo on the truck.
  • For attendees who acquired strong local brands, this is permission to stop second-guessing a rebrand they did not want to do anyway.
  • The exception is commercial and national-account motions, where a single brand still carries more weight.
DAY 1 RECAP

Brand Is The Hedge Against The AI Search Era

  • Paid demand-capture channels are getting more expensive and less efficient. The room flagged this pattern consistently.
  • Brand strength changes the question from "can I win the auction" to "am I the name the customer searches for in the first place."
  • AI-driven search and assistant behavior accelerates the value of being a known brand in a category.
  • Brand investment, traditionally hard to defend on a ROAS basis, is becoming easier to justify as a long-term efficiency play.
  • Marketing leaders should expect to be asked about brand strength in diligence, not just performance metrics.
DAY 1 RECAP

Capacity, Not Weather, Is The Real Constraint

These businesses are weather-enhanced, not weather-dependent. Resilience is a story about your operating system, not your forecast.

  • A useful operator framing was that these businesses are weather-enhanced, not weather-dependent.
  • When demand spikes or dips, the questions investors care about are which levers you can pull and how fast, not whether the weather cooperated.
  • Several attendees described running a small, well-understood set of demand levers they can move within days, rather than a sprawling set of tactics that are slow to activate.
  • Investors do not need to see the minutiae of how you respond to a weather shock. They need to see that you can.
  • The deeper point is that resilience is a story about your operating system, not your forecast.
DAY 1 RECAP

Marketing And Sales Show Up Together Or Not At All

  • A pattern that surfaced in debrief was that the strongest organizations have marketing and sales leaders presenting a single, synchronized scorecard.
  • Several attendees described their best board moments as the ones where lead generation and lead conversion were discussed as one shared accountability, not two adjacent ones.
  • The practical fix is to align on what marketing realistically owns and what the rest of the revenue motion owns, before targets are set, not after.
  • This reduces the defensive posture that emerges when marketing is held to a revenue number it cannot deliver alone.
  • For investor conversations, a unified marketing and sales story is significantly more credible than two separate ones.
DAY 1 RECAP

What To Expect When Buyers Show Up

  • Attendees were told to expect a real seat at the table in any future process, particularly in management presentations and follow-on diligence.
  • The typical exposure is a focused marketing section, often five to ten pages, with the marketing leader walking buyers through the funnel and the data.
  • The level to pitch at is 201, not 401. Be ready to tell the story before being asked to defend the deepest detail.
  • The room was advised to start building the data room and the narrative now, not in the weeks before a process kicks off.
  • A useful action item: rehearse the marketing story you would tell a buyer today, and use the gaps in that story to set your roadmap.
DAY 1 RECAP

What Surprised The Room

  • Several attendees flagged the percent-of-revenue-to-dollar-in-dollar-out reframe as the single most useful shift of the day.
  • The depth of capital rotating into physical services from AI-exposed software categories was newer information for parts of the room.
  • The death of the mono-brand mandate was a real shift for operators who had been carrying that as an assumed buyer requirement.
  • The permission to invest more aggressively, conditional on a credible attribution model, was a useful unlock for operators sitting on efficient funnels.
  • The framing of the marketing leader as a steward of growth capital, rather than a cost center, was the line that several attendees said they were taking back to their teams.
DAY 1 / KEY TAKEAWAYS

Six takeaways to tell your team on Monday.

  • Tell the organic growth story first; M&A is no longer the headline.
  • Manage marketing as capital invested, not as a percent of revenue.
  • Earn the right to invest more by closing the attribution gap to a workable confidence level.
  • Stitch demand generation to service-board capacity, and treat that habit as part of the operating system.
  • Protect local brand equity; build a credible brand story for the AI search era.
  • Show up to buyer conversations as a synchronized marketing and sales story, and start building that narrative now.
